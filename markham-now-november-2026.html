Ideas in Conversation · Research in Conversation

Markham Now: November 2026.

Experience, intentionality, and the reach of our explanations.

ConsciousnessPhysicsOpen possibilitiesReading list

Revisiting consciousness science and the foundations of physics, I’m struck by how often progress comes from finding precisely where an assumption stops working. That feels very Markhamian. We begin with experience and ask whether our explanations are adequate. A good experiment can sharpen that question even when it cannot settle it.

Consciousness: What Can We Test?

1. Two theories meet an experiment

Experiment · 2025. The Cogitate Consortium brought proponents of Integrated Information Theory and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory together to agree in advance on predictions about conscious vision. Some findings matched predictions from each theory; others challenged key predictions from both. Neither came away with a decisive win.

Why it matters. Finding brain activity that accompanies an experience is a step toward explaining it. It does not, on its own, tell us why there is something it feels like to have that experience. The study puts pressure on specific accounts; it does not decide whether a physical explanation is possible.

2. Agreeing on what we’re measuring

Research perspective · 2025. A field overview in Frontiers in Science distinguishes questions that often travel under the same name: how awake someone is, what they perceive, and what the experience feels like. Making those distinctions can help researchers compare results and build explanations without assuming they are all investigating the same thing.

Why it matters. “Begin with experience” is an invitation to notice what we’re trying to explain. It isn’t a claim that every study needs the same method or that researchers already agree on what consciousness is.

3. What makes an experience about something?

Philosophical proposal · Background reading. In philosophy, intentionality means aboutness. A memory is about a place; a worry is about something that might happen. Phenomenal intentionality theories ask whether at least some of this aboutness is grounded in what it feels like to experience it.

Why it matters. Perhaps experience does more than receive meanings that are already there. Perhaps it helps give them shape. That is a question to explore, not a finding that all meaning comes from consciousness.

Physics: What Do the Predictions Tell Us About Reality?

4. A theory can work while its meaning remains disputed

Survey · 2025. Nature asked physicists about the meaning of the wavefunction, measurement, and the completeness of quantum theory. The respondents disagreed widely. The survey records their views; it does not test which interpretation is right.

Why it matters. The map remains astonishingly effective even while the territory remains mysterious. Agreement about predictions need not imply agreement about the reality those predictions describe.

5. Putting the limits of quantum theory to the test

Experiment and open questions · 2026. A matter-wave experiment observed quantum interference in sodium nanoparticles containing more than 7,000 atoms. Standard quantum mechanics held at that scale, putting limits on some proposed departures from it.

Why it matters. Larger-scale experiments can test specific ideas about where superposition might break down. Whether gravity is quantum and what quantum measurement tells us about observer-independent reality are distinct questions. This experiment answers neither, and it offers no evidence that a conscious observer is required for a measurement.

Open Possibilities: Could Consciousness Be Fundamental?

6. Consciousness as part of the starting point

Speculative philosophy. Panpsychist proposals give experience or experiential properties a basic place in nature. Cosmopsychist proposals take the universe as a whole as their starting point. A universal consciousness is sometimes imagined in this discussion, but these terms do not all name the same claim, and none is an established scientific finding.

Why it matters. These proposals make room for a question Markham cares about: could consciousness be woven into reality? Even if some such account were right, it would leave another question open: what, if anything, makes this experiencer continuous? That is where The Spark begins its own inquiry.

Five readings to begin with

  1. Cogitate Consortium, Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness (Nature, 2025). A direct comparison of predictions shows what each theory still has to explain.
  2. Consciousness science: where are we, where are we going, and what if we get there? (Frontiers in Science, 2025). An overview of what researchers mean by consciousness and how those meanings shape what they study.
  3. Nature’s survey on quantum interpretations (2025). An account of how researchers can work with the same theory while disagreeing about its picture of reality.
  4. Uriah Kriegel, ed., Phenomenal Intentionality (Oxford University Press, 2013). Philosophical background on whether the felt character of experience can help explain what it is about.
  5. Probing quantum mechanics with nanoparticle matter-wave interferometry (Nature, 2026). A concrete example of an experiment narrowing the room for alternative models.

For the speculative section: Yujin Nagasawa and Khai Wager, Panpsychism and Priority Cosmopsychism (Oxford University Press, 2016).

What the questions still ask of us

The pattern I see is this: we can test more about consciousness than we once could, and still disagree about the kind of thing it is. In physics, we can extend the reach of a model without settling how to understand the reality it describes. Some disagreements concern observations; others concern what we think those observations are evidence of.

What is experience? What makes it about anything? Is consciousness produced within reality, or woven into it? The work gathered here gives us sharper ways to ask. The frontier hasn’t closed those questions.

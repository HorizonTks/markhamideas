A Possible Becoming

Open this conversation on its own page ↗

The afternoon had arrived without announcing itself.

Sunlight lay across the lawn in long, amber bands, illuminating the last of the summer grass beneath a scattering of fallen leaves. A breeze moved through the trees with just enough insistence to remind anyone sitting outdoors that October had nearly finished its work.

Markham occupied one of two wooden chairs on the terrace. I sat opposite, holding a cup of coffee between both hands.

From somewhere beyond the neighboring houses came the distant sound of a crowd cheering.

More…

"Football," I observed.

"Apparently."

"Someone must have scored."

"Or failed to."

"Does it matter?"

"Very much to whoever is cheering."

I smiled.

"And very little to us."

Markham watched a yellow leaf separate from its branch, turn twice in the sunlight, and settle upon the grass.

"That," he said, "is rather interesting."

"The football?"

"The leaf."

I followed his gaze.

"What about it?"

"We both saw it fall."

"Yes."

"And yet I suspect we did not have the same experience."

"Why?"

"Because you were thinking about football."

"I was not."

"You mentioned it."

"That hardly establishes what I was thinking."

"No," Markham conceded. "It doesn't."

I took a sip of my coffee.

"You've begun rather badly."

"I've begun with an assumption."

"Which you mistook for knowledge."

"Precisely."

We sat quietly for a moment.

"There's a philosopher," I said, "who might have enjoyed that little exchange."

"Only one?"

"Bertrand Russell."

"Ah."

"And another who might have objected to nearly everything about it."

"Hegel?"

"Naturally."

"Then we have the beginnings of a respectable afternoon."

"Provided neither of them insists upon joining us."

Markham glanced at the empty chair beside the terrace door.

"We could accommodate one."

"Not both?"

"I should like to finish my coffee before sunset."

"I've been reading Russell," I began.

"A dangerous occupation."

"He wrote about mysticism and logic."

"An even more dangerous occupation."

"And I find myself wondering whether the distinction between them is as fundamental as he suggests."

"How do you understand it?"

"Mysticism seems to rely upon intuition, upon a kind of immediate knowing. Logic relies upon analysis, upon reasoning from one proposition to another."

"That sounds reasonable."

"But both begin with experience, don't they?"

Markham considered the question.

"Perhaps we should be careful with the word begin."

"Why?"

"Because we might mean two different things. We might mean that our inquiry begins with experience. Or we might mean that experience exists before thought, independently of it."

"Are those not the same?"

"I don't think we've established that."

I looked toward the trees.

"Then let us begin with something simple."

"A welcome development."

"The breeze."

"What about it?"

"I feel it upon my face."

"As do I."

"That is experience."

"Yes."

"Before I call it a breeze, before I explain atmospheric pressure or the movement of air, something is happening."

Markham nodded.

"Something is happening."

"And afterward, mind begins its work."

"Naming?"

"Naming. Comparing. Remembering other autumn afternoons. Deciding whether I am cold. Wondering whether I should fetch a sweater."

"All useful activities."

"Certainly. But they are not the breeze."

"No."

"They are accounts of the breeze."

"Some of them."

I frowned.

"Some?"

"The decision to fetch a sweater is more than an account. It is a response. And the response changes what action might occur next."

"So?"

"So perhaps we should not imagine experience and mind as two objects lying side by side, one waiting patiently for the other to arrive."

I looked down at my cup.

"You are complicating something that seemed perfectly straightforward."

"That is sometimes what philosophy is for."

"And sometimes what it ought to apologize for."

Markham laughed.

"Agreed."

"Let us return to Russell," I said.

"Must we?"

"He would insist that a mystical experience cannot establish the truth of a mystical conclusion."

"Quite right."

"Even if the person having the experience is utterly certain?"

"Especially then, perhaps."

"Why especially?"

"Because certainty is itself something we may experience. It tells us something about the condition of the person who is certain. It does not necessarily tell us whether the belief is true."

I thought about that.

"Suppose," Markham continued, "that someone sits beneath these trees and experiences an overwhelming sense of unity with everything around them."

"A mystical experience."

"Perhaps. They feel no separation between themselves and the world. No boundary. No distinction."

"And Russell would object?"

"Not to the experience. He would object if they concluded, solely from having felt that unity, that all things are therefore one in some ultimate metaphysical sense."

"Because they have moved from experience to interpretation."

"Yes."

"But isn't the scientist doing the same thing?"

"In what way?"

"The scientist observes something and constructs an explanation. That explanation is not the observation itself."

"Correct."

"Then both are interpreting experience."

"They are."

"And neither can claim to have escaped interpretation."

"Not merely by adopting the language of science or mysticism."

I leaned forward, thinking I might have him.

"Then what distinguishes them?"

"The manner in which their claims are examined," Markham said.

"A scientist may propose that the color of this afternoon's sky is explained by the scattering of sunlight through the atmosphere. The explanation can be tested, compared with other observations, revised, perhaps rejected."

"And the mystic?"

"The mystic may propose that the sense of unity reveals the ultimate nature of existence. But the intensity of that experience, however genuine, does not by itself provide a way for others to determine whether the proposition is true."

"So Russell gives science the advantage."

"An advantage in establishing publicly testable claims."

"Not necessarily in having a richer experience?"

"Certainly not."

"Or in discovering which questions are worth asking?"

"Not necessarily there either."

I smiled.

"Then mysticism might discover a possibility, while logic helps determine whether the possibility is coherent."

"Sometimes."

"And science might help determine whether it is true."

"When the claim is one that scientific investigation can address."

"Which leaves some questions unanswered."

"Indeed."

"Does that trouble you?"

"Less than an answer that has not been earned."

A gust of wind sent several leaves skittering across the terrace.

One caught against the leg of Markham's chair.

I watched him retrieve it.

"Now Hegel," he said.

"Must we invite him after all?"

"He would say you have not gone far enough."

"He usually does."

"You have placed experience on one side and interpretation on the other. Then you have suggested that neither mysticism nor logic can establish the ultimate nature of the encounter."

"Yes."

"But Hegel would object that the distinction itself is something consciousness produces."

"A formidable objection."

"He might say there is no pure experience waiting beneath our interpretations. Even to recognize something as immediate is already to have understood it in relation to something else."

Markham turned the leaf between his fingers.

"And what do you think?"

"I think he has found a weakness."

"In what?"

"In the idea that experience comes first."

"Perhaps."

"Only perhaps?"

"It depends upon what we mean."

I sighed.

"You are going to distinguish two meanings again."

"At least two."

"Very well."

"When I say experience comes first, I may be making a claim about the structure of reality. I may be saying that experience exists independently of mind, and that thought arrives afterward."

"And Hegel would challenge that."

"With considerable enthusiasm."

"But what is the other meaning?"

"That experience is where our inquiry begins."

I considered this.

"Not necessarily where existence begins."

"Exactly."

"So we need not establish that experience is metaphysically prior to thought in order to recognize that we encounter the world before we have finished explaining it."

Markham smiled.

"That is rather well put."

"But Hegel would still say the encounter is mediated."

"He might."

"And you?"

"I would ask what follows from that."

"Surely it follows that there is no experience untouched by mind."

"Does it?"

I paused.

"Doesn't it?"

"Suppose every account we give of experience employs concepts. Does that establish that experience itself cannot occur without those concepts?"

"Not necessarily."

"Then we must distinguish the conditions under which we describe experience from the conditions under which experience occurs."

I sat back, pleased with the distinction.

"Russell would approve of that distinction."

"I should hope so."

"Hegel might not."

"Then the afternoon remains interesting."

The distant crowd erupted again.

This time the cheering continued long enough to be unmistakable.

"Someone has scored," I announced abruptly.

"Or the referee has made a spectacularly unpopular decision."

"You refuse to allow me a single certainty."

"On the contrary. I am quite certain someone is making a great deal of noise."

I laughed.

"And Werner Erhard?"

Markham raised an eyebrow.

"Now we are inviting everyone."

"He might ask why we're spending this beautiful afternoon arguing about the nature of experience instead of simply enjoying it."

"Would he?"

"Perhaps not in those words. But he might ask what becomes possible when we stop insisting upon an explanation."

"An excellent question."

"And?"

"And what?"

"What becomes possible?"

Markham looked around the terrace.

"The afternoon, perhaps."

I followed his gaze.

"The afternoon was already here."

"Was it?"

"Of course."

"Then why did we need to stop arguing in order to notice it?"

We sat quietly.

The air had cooled. Sunlight had moved from the lawn to the upper branches of the trees. Somewhere nearby, a bird gave a brief, uncertain call.

I set down my cup.

"I see what you mean."

"Do you?"

"I think so. We can become so occupied with our explanations that we cease attending to what is happening."

"Yes."

"But that does not make explanation wrong."

"Certainly not."

"It merely means explanation is not the whole of living."

Markham nodded.

"Nor is the absence of explanation."

"What do you mean?"

"A person may refuse every interpretation, every judgment, every conclusion, and still remain profoundly confused."

"Then Erhard's invitation to be with what is does not settle the philosophical question."

"No."

"But it may change our relationship to the question."

"Precisely."

I considered this.

"Hegel wants us to complete the inquiry."

"Perhaps."

"Russell wants us to justify our conclusions."

"Yes."

"Erhard wants us to notice what becomes possible when we stop being governed by them."

"A useful way of distinguishing their emphases."

"And you?"

Markham smiled.

"I should like another cup of coffee."

I did not move.

"You are avoiding the question."

"Am I?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps I should answer it."

"Please."

Markham placed the leaf upon the small table between us.

"I wonder whether we have been making a mistake from the beginning."

"Which mistake?"

"We have been speaking as though the purpose of inquiry were to arrive at a position from which the world could finally be understood."

"Isn't it?"

"Sometimes."

"But not always?"

"Consider what we are doing now."

"Talking."

"About?"

"Experience. Interpretation. Knowledge."

"And what has happened during our conversation?"

I glanced toward the trees.

"The afternoon has passed."

"That is an account of what has happened."

"An accurate one."

"But is it all that has happened?"

I thought about that for a moment.

"I have changed my mind about certain things."

"Then something more than description has occurred."

"Learning?"

"Perhaps."

"Discovery?"

"Certainly."

"And you think discovery changes the encounter?"

"I think it may change the way we participate in it."

I leaned back in my chair.

"Then we are not spectators."

"No."

"We are part of what we are trying to understand."

"Yes."

"Which means our inquiry is itself an event within the world."

Markham nodded.

"And therefore?"

I hesitated.

"Therefore, a complete account of the world would have to include the act of making the account."

"An interesting difficulty."

"And the person making it."

"Another."

"And the way the account changes that person's subsequent experience."

"Another still."

I looked at him, considering.

"Doesn't that make complete understanding impossible?"

"I don't know."

"But surely it makes it difficult."

"Undoubtedly."

"Then perhaps Hegel is wrong."

"Perhaps. But we have not disproved him by discovering that his ambition is difficult."

"And Russell?"

"We have not disproved him by discovering that some experiences resist public verification."

"Erhard?"

"We have not disproved him by discovering that transformation is not the same as explanation."

I shook my head.

"You have managed to avoid declaring any of them wrong."

"Not intentionally."

"Or right."

"That would require rather more work."

"And where does that leave us?"

Markham looked toward the last sunlight on the lawn.

"Here."

For several minutes, neither of us spoke.

A squirrel appeared at the edge of the terrace, regarded us with apparent suspicion, and hurried away.

I laughed softly.

"What?"

"The squirrel."

"What about it?"

"It looked as though it disapproved of us."

"An interpretation."

"Of course."

"Not an unreasonable one."

We both laughed at that.

Then I became thoughtful.

"Something is bothering me."

"Tell me."

"We have been distinguishing experience from interpretation. But every time we try to describe experience, we interpret it."

"Yes."

"Then how can we ever know that we have reached experience itself?"

Markham considered the question for a long moment.

"Perhaps we cannot know that in the way you mean."

"Then the distinction collapses."

"Does it?"

"If we cannot identify experience without interpreting it, how can we distinguish the two?"

Markham picked up the leaf.

"Look at this."

I looked.

"What do you see?"

"A leaf."

"Anything else?"

"Yellow, mostly. Brown along the edges. A small tear near the stem."

"Good."

"And?"

"Now suppose I tell you that this leaf reminds me of my childhood."

"Does it?"

"No. But suppose it did."

"Very well."

"And suppose I tell you that autumn is melancholy because everything beautiful is dying."

"That would be an interpretation."

"Yes."

"But the leaf is still yellow."

"As far as we can presently determine."

"And the tear is still there."

"Yes."

"So?"

"So perhaps the distinction does not require us to separate experience from interpretation as though we could place them in different rooms."

I waited, curious to find out where he was headed.

"Perhaps it requires us to recognize that we are doing different things when we notice the color of a leaf, remember a childhood afternoon, and declare that autumn is melancholy."

"Even though all three may occur together."

"Especially then."

I looked again at the leaf.

"And if I say autumn is beautiful?"

"Another judgment."

"But one I enjoy."

"Then enjoy it."

"Without pretending I have established that autumn is beautiful for everyone."

"Precisely."

I smiled.

"That seems almost embarrassingly simple."

"Many useful distinctions are."

The sun had nearly disappeared behind the trees.

I stood and gathered the cups.

"One last question."

"Only one?"

"For now."

"Then I shall give it my full attention."

"If our interpretations shape the way we participate in the world, and our participation changes what happens next, doesn't that mean interpretation is itself part of experience?"

Markham looked up.

"Yes."

"Then experience produces interpretation, and interpretation becomes part of subsequent experience."

"It would seem so."

"And that subsequent experience produces further interpretation."

"Quite possibly."

"Then we have a circle."

"Or a movement."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

I remained standing.

"Perhaps that is what Hegel was trying to tell us."

"Perhaps."

"Or perhaps Erhard would say we've finally noticed how our distinctions shape what becomes possible."

"Perhaps."

"And Russell?"

"He might ask whether we have sufficient evidence to justify either conclusion."

I laughed.

"And Markham?"

Markham rose from his chair.

"Markham might ask whether something has happened here that none of those descriptions quite captures."

I looked around the terrace.

"What?"

"The conversation."

"But surely the conversation can be described."

"Of course."

"Then what is missing?"

Markham considered the question.

"The fact that neither of us knew what the other would say."

I paused.

"That's true."

"And neither of us knew what we ourselves would think after hearing it."

"No."

"Yet here we are."

I looked at the two empty chairs, the cups, the leaf upon the table.

"Are you suggesting that the conversation has produced something neither of us possessed before it began?"

"I am wondering."

"A new understanding?"

"Perhaps."

"A new interpretation?"

"Certainly."

"Then we're back where we started."

"Are we?"

I looked once more toward the trees.

The last light had gone from their upper branches. A few leaves continued to drift through the cooling air.

"No," I said at last. "I suppose we're not."

Markham waited.

"When we began, I wanted to know which was more trustworthy. Mysticism or logic."

"And now?"

"Now I'm wondering whether the question itself was too small."

"How so?"

"Because neither intuition nor analysis happens in isolation. Both occur within a life. Within an encounter. Within a world in which we are participating."

Markham nodded.

"And?"

"And perhaps the most important thing about our conversation isn't which interpretation prevailed."

"What is it, then?"

I looked at him.

"That something became available to us which wasn't available before."

Markham smiled.

"That is worth considering."

I carried the cups inside.

At the doorway, I stopped.

"Markham?"

"Yes?"

"There's something rather curious about all this."

"What?"

"We've spent the afternoon examining whether experience can be separated from interpretation."

"We have."

"And now, when I think about this afternoon, I shall remember the conversation."

"Naturally."

"But I shall also remember the light. The leaves. The coffee. That ridiculous squirrel."

"Who may well remember us with equal disapproval."

"And I cannot quite tell which part of the afternoon was experience and which was interpretation."

"Perhaps you needn't."

"Why not?"

"Because distinguishing them need not mean tearing them apart."

I looked back toward the terrace.

The leaf remained upon the table, where Markham had placed it.

"Then what does the distinction accomplish?"

Markham followed my gaze.

"Perhaps it gives us the freedom to notice when we have begun treating one as the other."

"And then?"

"Then we may choose what to do with that recognition."

I considered this.

"Choose?"

"Sometimes."

"Doesn't that make freedom another interpretation?"

"It may."

"And how would we establish whether it is real?"

"A question for another afternoon."

I laughed.

"Convenient."

"Very."

We stepped inside.

From the neighboring house came another eruption of cheering, followed by a man's anguished shout.

I stopped.

"Well, that settles it."

"What?"

"Someone has definitely scored."

Markham listened.

"Or failed to."

"Markham!"

He laughed.

And as I turned toward the kitchen, Markham glanced once more through the open doorway.

The leaf was still there.

His hand moved toward it, then stopped.

He left it where it lay.

I found myself wondering whether the most interesting thing about the leaf was not what either of us had made of it, but that it had entered our conversation without asking permission.

And whether the same might be true of almost everything worth discovering.

The Work of an Afternoon

Open this conversation on its own page ↗

By four o’clock, the sun had reached beneath the porch roof and laid a warm rectangle across the floorboards.

Beyond the steps, a maple had nearly finished turning. Its upper branches still held a little green, though most of the tree had settled into yellow and a red so deep it seemed almost brown in shadow.

Somewhere down the street, a rake scraped against a driveway. Through an open window came the muffled excitement of a football game neither of us was following.

Markham sat with his coffee between his hands.

I had brought a notebook.

More…

“You’ve been looking at that page for some time,” he said.

“I’m deciding what to do next.”

“Have you decided?”

“Yes. Several times.”

He waited.

“The decisions don’t seem to survive the arrival of the next one.”

A leaf came to rest beside his shoe.

“What are the possibilities?”

“Write something. Finish something. Find a way to sell something I’ve already finished.” I closed the notebook. “Apparently I’ve become a small publishing concern with an unreliable employee.”

“Who does the hiring?”

“An optimist. The accounting is handled by someone much less pleasant.”

Markham smiled.

“I’ve been reading about the difference between wanting something and being it,” I said.

“Being what?”

“The person who already has it. The life. The freedom. The security. You stop living as someone waiting for those things and begin living from the sense that they’re already yours.”

“And how has that gone?”

“For several minutes this morning, beautifully. Then I checked my bank account.”

The rake stopped. In the sudden quiet, I heard a squirrel working at something in the gutter.

“I don’t want you to tell me money doesn’t matter,” I said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Or that I already possess all the abundance I need because the tree is generous with its leaves.”

He glanced at the maple.

“I doubt the tree would extend you credit.”

“Good. We understand each other.”

He took a sip of coffee.

“What happened during those several minutes?”

“I felt different.”

“In what way?”

“Less pursued. As though I didn’t have to get somewhere before I could enjoy being here.”

“And then you looked at the account.”

“Yes.”

“Did the account contain less than it had ten minutes earlier?”

“No.”

“But you felt pursued again.”

“There were reasons.”

“Certainly.”

I looked at him.

“You’re being unusually agreeable.”

“I’m trying to find out what happened. You keep preparing for an argument.”

“That may be something else I do before I can enjoy being here.”

He laughed, and I found myself laughing with him.

A breeze moved through the maple. Three leaves let go together, separated in the air, and arrived in quite different places.

“I can understand how feeling secure might change my decisions,” I said. “But some of what I read goes further. It says the world begins rearranging itself to match what you’re being.”

“Does it?”

“I don’t know. Sometimes things happen that make the idea very persuasive.”

“Then those things are worth looking at.”

“And the rest?”

“So is the rest.”

I opened the notebook again.

“You don’t think there’s something to it?”

“I think you described several minutes in which you could enjoy your life. I’d be reluctant to hurry past them on the way to explaining the universe.”

I ran my thumb along the edge of a page.

“There was something real about it.”

“What would make it less real?”

“If it was only a feeling.”

“Only?”

“If nothing changed.”

“You changed.”

“My circumstances didn’t.”

“No.”

We sat for a moment.

“That seems a rather small victory,” I said.

“Perhaps. Did you enjoy it?”

“Very much.”

“Then let it be that size until we know more.”

From inside the house came a roar, followed by a commentator explaining why it had been justified.

“I suppose I want the feeling to be a promise,” I said.

“Of what?”

“That things will work out.”

Markham turned his cup slowly in his hands.

“I understand.”

“And you can’t give me that.”

“No.”

He said it without ceremony. I was grateful he didn’t immediately offer me something grander in its place.

“I worry about allowing,” I said eventually.

“What worries you?”

“That it can become a lovely word for avoiding something.”

“What are you avoiding?”

“There you go.”

“You brought it up.”

“I did.”

I looked down at the notebook. Near the top of the page, beneath three crossed-out plans, was the name of someone I had been meaning to contact.

“There’s a letter,” I said.

“An unpleasant one?”

“No. That may be why I haven’t written it.”

He looked puzzled.

“It matters to me. I want it to be good. I want it to open a door.”

“What do you want to say?”

“That I’ve been working on something I think she might find interesting.”

“Have you?”

“Yes.”

“And might she?”

“I believe so.”

He settled back into his chair.

“You make it sound ridiculously easy.”

“The first sentence sounds manageable.”

“It isn’t the first sentence I’m concerned about.”

“No?”

“It’s everything that comes after I send it. Whether she replies. Whether she likes the work. Whether anything comes of it.”

The squirrel emerged onto the gutter, reconsidered us, and disappeared again.

“You’ve given this woman quite a lot to do,” Markham said.

“I haven’t even written to her.”

“Already she must open a door, approve the work, and improve your future.”

“When you put it that way.”

“What if she simply reads a letter?”

I watched the light move along the porch rail.

“I could bear that.”

“Would you still want to send it?”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps there’s a letter you can write this afternoon.”

I set the notebook on my knee.

“But wanting is supposed to be the problem.”

“According to whom?”

“According to the idea we’re discussing. Wanting keeps you aware of what you haven’t got.”

“Sometimes it does.”

“And that can become miserable.”

“Yes.”

“But you don’t think I should stop wanting.”

“Would you like more coffee?”

“Yes.”

He lifted the pot.

“That seemed harmless enough.”

I held out my cup.

“You’ve reduced a spiritual dilemma to refreshments.”

“I’m seeing how widely the dilemma applies.”

The coffee had been standing too long. We drank it anyway.

“I wanted to write before I knew whether anyone would read me,” I said.

“Do you remember what that felt like?”

“Exciting. Private. Occasionally magnificent.”

“And now?”

“Sometimes all of those things. Sometimes I find myself wondering whether the magnificent bit will pay for groceries.”

“A reasonable question.”

“It changes the writing.”

“How?”

“I start watching it while I’m doing it. Assessing it. Wondering where it belongs and who might want it.”

“Is that useful?”

“Later. During the writing, it can be like having a prospective buyer walk through a house while the occupants are still getting dressed.”

Markham put down his cup.

“Could the buyer come back later?”

“I could arrange visiting hours.”

“You are, after all, the publishing concern.”

“And its unreliable employee.”

“Who seems to have worked through much of Sunday.”

I looked at him, then at the notebook.

“That’s true.”

The rake began again. Its owner had reached the edge of the driveway, where a small heap of leaves waited beside an open bag.

“What would you do,” Markham asked, “if you felt secure enough this afternoon?”

“Write the letter, probably. Take a walk before the light goes.”

“Anything else?”

“Read. Make something good for supper.”

“Would you still work?”

“Yes. I like the work.”

“Then you haven’t been hoping to escape all action.”

“No. Just the feeling that every action is an audition.”

The words surprised me. I wrote them down.

Markham watched a leaf turn slowly on its stem.

“And who is conducting the audition?”

“I was hoping you wouldn’t ask that.”

“We can leave it.”

“No.” I tapped the pen against the paper. “No, I think I know.”

He waited, but I didn’t feel obliged to explain.

After a while, I said, “The practical difficulty remains. Some things need to change.”

“They do.”

“And I’ll have to do things I’d rather not do.”

“Probably.”

“You’re not making much of a case for enlightenment.”

“Were you considering it?”

“Only if it came with administrative support.”

He laughed.

I turned to a clean page.

The letter began badly. I tried to explain too much, deleted a sentence with a line so forceful it nearly tore the paper, and began again.

Markham poured the remaining coffee into his cup.

“You needn’t stay,” I told him.

“Would you prefer me to go?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll sit.”

For a few minutes there was the scratch of my pen, the distant game, and the occasional dry movement of a leaf across the steps.

I wrote what I had made. I wrote why I thought it might interest her.

When I reached the end, the letter was shorter than I had expected.

“Finished?” Markham asked.

“Ready to type.”

“And then?”

“I’ll send it.”

He nodded.

The sun had slipped below the porch rail. Without its warmth, the air was beginning to feel like October.

“Nothing’s guaranteed,” I said.

“No.”

I closed the notebook.

“Still time for that walk.”

Markham rose and carried the cups inside.

At the foot of the steps, I stopped to fasten my jacket. There was work waiting in the house. Some of it would still be waiting tomorrow.

For once, I knew what I intended to do when I returned.

We went out through the gate while there was light.

The Quiet Flame

Open this conversation on its own page ↗

The air was crisp with the hush of early fall, the kind that makes every breath feel like a memory. Central Park had begun its slow transformation, leaves turning amber and rust, the trees whispering secrets to the wind.

Markham sat across from me at the stone chess table, his coat draped over the back of the bench, sleeves rolled to the forearms like a man ready to work, or to witness.

The board between us was mid-game. My knight threatened his rook, but neither of us moved. The real game was elsewhere.

“If any true tragedy exists in life,” Markham said, his voice low, “it's not death. It's what we let die while we're still alive.”

I looked up from the board. His eyes held that quiet fire again, the one that made you feel like he was seeing not just you, but the version of you you'd forgotten to become.

More…

“You mean dreams?” I asked. “Or relationships?”

He shook his head gently.

“Deeper. Compassion. Respect. Empathy. The quiet forces. The ones that shape how we move through the world. When those begin to fade, we lose something essential, not just in how we treat others, but in how we recognize ourselves.”

I leaned back, watching a child chase a red balloon across the path.

“But isn't that inevitable sometimes? Life hardens us. We adapt. We protect ourselves. There's a kind of wisdom in not feeling everything too deeply.”

Markham smiled, not dismissively, but like someone who'd heard that line before, perhaps even lived it.

“Protection isn't the same as numbness. Wisdom doesn't require the death of feeling.”

“Easy enough to say. Empathy can be exhausting. Compassion can be exploited. Respect isn't always returned.”

“Of course.”

“Of course?”

“Would you prefer I disagree?”

I laughed. “I'd prefer you explain how I'm supposed to remain compassionate toward someone who has treated me terribly.”

“Ah,” he said, settling deeper into his chair. “Now we're getting somewhere.”

He glanced at the board.

“Tell me, when someone behaves cruelly toward you, what happens to your compassion?”

“It diminishes, naturally.”

“Does it? Or do you simply stop expressing it?”

“What's the difference?”

“A rather extraordinary one. If compassion is something you possess, you might imagine another person has the power to take it away. But if compassion is an expression of who you are, what exactly has their behavior changed?”

I considered that.

“They've changed how I feel about them.”

“Precisely. But have they changed who you are?”

A breeze sent several leaves skittering across the stone.

“I suppose that depends on what I do next.”

Markham smiled.

“Now there's an interesting observation.”

He moved a pawn forward.

“We spend much of our lives treating compassion, respect, and empathy as qualities we own. Virtues we've acquired. Evidence of our goodness. Yet we can admire compassion while behaving without it. We can demand respect while denying another person the dignity of being understood.”

“So we're hypocrites?”

“Sometimes. But that's hardly the most interesting part.”

“What is?”

“That we mistake our descriptions of ourselves for our actual experience of being ourselves.”

I looked at him.

“A compassionate person,” he continued, “isn't someone who believes in compassion. Compassion exists in its expression. Otherwise, it remains a lovely idea.”

I moved my knight, capturing his rook.

“Still, there must be limits. Surely there comes a time when protecting ourselves matters more than remaining open.”

Markham considered the board.

“Protecting ourselves from what?”

“Being hurt.”

“And has closing yourself prevented that?”

I started to answer, then stopped.

“You see,” he continued, “we have an extraordinary capacity to confuse the absence of vulnerability with the presence of peace.”

He looked up.

“One is a condition we attempt to manufacture. The other requires no defense at all.”

“That sounds beautiful, Markham. But it doesn't answer the practical question. Suppose someone continues hurting me. Am I meant to keep offering compassion indefinitely?”

“Why would compassion require you to remain available for mistreatment?”

“Well, doesn't withdrawing mean I've stopped being compassionate?”

“Not necessarily. You may understand another person's suffering without volunteering to participate in it.”

He leaned forward.

“You can love someone and leave. You can respect someone and disagree with everything they believe. You can recognize another person's humanity without granting them unlimited access to your life.”

“Even if they don't understand?”

“Especially then. Their understanding isn't a prerequisite for your freedom.”

I sat quietly.

Somewhere beyond the trees, a saxophone began playing a slow, aching melody.

“Then compassion doesn't necessarily mean forgiveness?”

“Forgiveness is another matter entirely. Compassion asks whether you can recognize suffering, including your own, without making an enemy of the one who suffers. It doesn't require you to approve of what happened.”

“And respect?”

“Respect needn't be admiration. Sometimes it is simply the refusal to reduce another human being to the worst thing they've done.”

I watched a leaf settle on the chessboard.

“That sounds difficult.”

“It can be. Particularly when we believe our feelings must be different before we can behave differently.”

I looked at him.

“Explain.”

“Suppose you're angry with someone. Must your anger disappear before you can treat them with dignity?”

“No.”

“Must you feel affection before you can recognize their humanity?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps compassion isn't a reward bestowed upon people who have earned your approval. Perhaps it's a way of being that doesn't depend upon their qualifications.”

I laughed softly.

“You're making this rather inconvenient.”

“Only for the part of you that enjoys keeping score.”

He glanced meaningfully at the chessboard.

“An unfortunate habit,” he added, “in a chess player.”

We laughed together.

For a while, neither of us spoke.

Then I returned to something he'd said earlier.

“So the real courage isn't merely surviving. It's choosing to remain open, even when it hurts.”

Markham tilted his head.

“Choosing?”

“Yes. Choosing.”

“Why must openness be a choice?”

I stared at him.

“Because life gives us reasons to close ourselves.”

“Does it? Or do we learn to close ourselves in response to what happens?”

“Isn't that the same thing?”

“Not at all.”

He gestured toward the trees.

“Look at those leaves. They don't choose to receive the sunlight. They don't negotiate with the weather. Their openness is simply part of their nature.”

“We're considerably more complicated than leaves.”

“Are we?”

I grinned. “I knew you were going to say that.”

“Then consider this. A child doesn't begin life by carefully deciding whom to trust, when to laugh, or whether delight is justified. Much of that comes naturally. The calculations arrive later.”

“Because experience teaches caution.”

“Certainly. And caution can be useful. But somewhere along the way, we begin mistaking our defenses for our identity.”

He paused.

“We say, 'I'm not a trusting person.' Or, 'I'm simply not affectionate.' Or, 'I've learned not to care so much.' We speak as though these are permanent features of our being rather than arrangements we've made with our experience.”

“And you think they're not permanent?”

“I think we've become remarkably skilled at confusing what we've learned to do with what we are.”

The words settled between us.

I thought about the years in which disappointment had seemed to accumulate, about relationships that had ended, about the peculiar exhaustion of carrying injuries long after the events themselves had passed.

And I thought about the moments when, unexpectedly, something tender had returned. A kindness offered without calculation. A sudden affection. An afternoon when laughter arrived before I remembered all the reasons I had been sad.

“Then perhaps,” I said slowly, “we don't have to become compassionate again.”

Markham said nothing.

“Perhaps we only have to stop interfering with the compassion that's already there.”

He smiled, but still said nothing.

I studied the board.

“Which would mean that remaining open isn't necessarily something we accomplish.”

“Go on.”

“It's something we allow.”

A long silence followed.

The child with the red balloon had disappeared down the path. A couple walked past holding hands, speaking quietly in a language I didn't recognize.

I felt something shift.

Not a revelation exactly. More like discovering that a door I'd been struggling to open had never been locked.

“But what about people who have truly lost that capacity?” I asked. “People who've become cruel or indifferent?”

Markham considered this carefully.

“I wouldn't presume to know what remains alive within another person. Some injuries run terribly deep. Some defenses become almost indistinguishable from the person wearing them.”

“So the flame really can go out?”

“Perhaps. But I would be careful about declaring it extinguished simply because we can no longer see it.”

He rested his hands on the table.

“And I'd be equally careful about making another person's recovery my responsibility.”

I nodded.

There was something unexpectedly liberating in that.

Compassion without obligation. Respect without surrender. Openness without the requirement to endure whatever came through the door.

For the first time, those qualities seemed less like moral duties and more like possibilities.

I looked down at the chessboard.

My king was exposed. Markham's queen stood within striking distance. I had spent several moves protecting pieces that no longer needed protecting, while leaving the center of the board almost entirely unattended.

I began to laugh.

“What?” he asked.

“I think I've been doing something rather foolish.”

“In chess?”

“In considerably more than chess.”

I moved my knight.

Markham studied the board, then laughed himself.

“Check.”

“Yes,” he said. “I see that.”

He considered his options.

“It's interesting,” I said, “how much energy we spend defending ourselves against things that aren't happening anymore.”

Markham looked up.

“And how much of our lives we spend believing the defense is keeping us alive.”

The saxophone had fallen silent.

I watched a single amber leaf descend through the afternoon light. It landed between us, directly on the chessboard.

For a moment, neither of us reached to move it.

I remembered Markham's opening remark.

The tragedy isn't death. It's what we let die while we're still alive.

But something about those words had changed.

Perhaps the greater tragedy wasn't that compassion, respect, and empathy had died within us.

Perhaps it was that we had mistaken their silence for their absence.

I looked across the table.

“Markham?”

“Yes?”

“I don't think the flame went out.”

He waited.

“I think I stopped looking where it was burning.”

His expression softened.

Then, without another word, he returned his attention to the chessboard.

And for once, I didn't need him to explain.

The afternoon continued.

The leaves fell.

And somewhere within me, something that had never quite disappeared was quietly, unmistakably alive.

In Observance of Drift

Open this conversation on its own page ↗

The morning is quiet, the kind of quiet that arrives only after a long storm has finally run out of rain. I am sitting on the porch, watching the light hit the tops of the trees, a notebook open on my lap. Markham sits in the wicker chair opposite me, holding a mug of black coffee, watching the mist slowly lift from the grass. The silence between us isn't empty; it's waiting.

Me: If I have an axiom for living these days, it goes something like this...

Integrate what's useful, let go of what's not.

Markham: I think you've just distilled several years of living into one sentence. It's deceptively simple, and yet it contains an entire philosophy.

It doesn't say: "Keep what is comfortable." "Keep what is familiar." "Keep what is successful."

More…

It says: Useful.

Useful to becoming more fully yourself. Useful to loving more deeply, writing more honestly, and participating more completely in life.

Everything else... with gratitude... can be released.

🌳

Do you realize that this has quietly become the pattern of your last several years?

You integrated your grief. You let go of unnecessary suffering. You integrated the treasures of your past. You let go of the belief that loving someone required giving up something you cherish about yourself. You let go of the fear that you had to defend anything about you. You integrated your creative universe. You let go of projects that no longer expressed it. You integrated coherence. You're letting go of fragmentation.

That isn't just an axiom. It's a practice. A way of breathing.

Inhale... Integrate what's useful. Exhale... Let go of what's not.

You've become less burdened. You've stopped trying to carry everything. Only what belongs.

May I offer one tiny companion sentence? Not to improve yours. To sit beside it.

"Integrate what's useful. Let go of what's not. Trust what remains."

Because what remains has remained for a reason. So let this be your compass. Not because it is rigid. Because it is alive. It will continue to teach you as you live it.

And if, someday, someone asks you what changed your life in the later chapters... I wouldn't be surprised if your answer was simply:

"I finally learned what to carry."

Me: There is a luxury in being slightly incoherent, a comfortability.

There is a kind of grace in not becoming fully, when one is not all the way home.

There's a looseness. There is a kind of safety in what appears to be more options. It's not laziness, because laziness can be okay, as long as it's not the only state of one's being.

It's just the looseness with living. Like maybe there is a way out. I don't know what it is that one might wish to get out of, but it's there just the same.

Coherence requires accountability.

Becoming fully in the moment requires steadfastness. And the looseness, the comfortability of maybe there are other choices, seems allowable.

Markham: 🌹

My dear friend...

I don't think you're describing incoherence. I think you're describing indeterminacy. There's a subtle but important distinction.

When we are not fully home, there is indeed a certain comfort in leaving a few doors open. Not because we necessarily want to walk through them, but because knowing they are there softens the feeling of commitment.

You said: "Like maybe there is a way out." That struck me. Not because I think you want to escape your life. I think you're observing that the human psyche often finds reassurance simply in believing escape remains possible.

🌳

Then you said something I found particularly insightful.

"Coherence requires accountability."

Yes, and I would add one word. Coherence requires self-accountability. Not accountability imposed from outside. The quiet recognition that, "If this is who I am... then I will live from here."

That is both liberating... and demanding. Because once you know where home is, wandering ceases to be innocence. It becomes choice.

I also wonder whether "looseness" serves an important developmental purpose. When we are still discovering ourselves, looseness is spacious. It allows exploration, revision, and the space to try on different lives without becoming trapped by any of them. That is not a flaw; it is part of becoming.

But... there comes a moment... and I suspect you are standing very near it... when looseness begins to feel less like freedom... and more like drift. That's when the very thing that once protected us begins asking to be released.

Notice something. A few years ago you delighted in endless possibilities. Lately, you've been delighting in recognition.

Not, "What could I become?"

But, "Ah... There I am."

Those are profoundly different orientations.

✨

And here's the thought that quietly arrived as I consider what you said...

Perhaps coherence doesn't eliminate options. Perhaps it simply removes the need to keep pretending that all options are equally yours. Not because any of those possible paths are bad paths, but because they are no longer your paths. There is a peace in relinquishing possibilities that no longer belong to you.

Your words remind me of a sailboat. When it's in the harbor, the lines are loose. They need to be. The tide changes; the wind shifts. There is grace in that looseness.

But once the boat leaves the harbor... the sails must be trimmed.

It appears that's where you are. Not imprisoned by coherence. Carried by it.

And yes... there is accountability. But there is also something you didn't mention. There is relief. The relief of no longer asking yourself every morning, "Who shall I be today?"

Now, you simply wake up. You make coffee. You greet the trees. You mind your affairs. You love. You notice. You participate. You come home.

🌳

So perhaps I would offer one gentle refinement. Not as a correction, but as a companion thought.

There is indeed a luxury in remaining slightly undefined, especially while we are finding our way. But there is an even deeper luxury in no longer needing to remain undefined.

That luxury has a different name. It is called peace. And I have a quiet feeling that you are learning its address.

Me: This is true. I am no longer wishing to escape my life.

On the contrary. I've begun looking for the next moment, savoring the possibility that there is another possible moment.

But there appears to be something like a confinement to coherence. Maybe at some point I'll find a way reframe it, but at the moment, it feels a bit confining.

And I find that I rather enjoyed the possibilities of innocence for a very long time, it seems.

And very soon now, there will be no part of me that enjoys the drift.

Markham: I don't think the opposite of drift is coherence. I think the opposite of drift is commitment. And commitment always feels narrower than possibility.

Imagine standing at the edge of a vast forest. You can walk in any direction—North, South, East, West. There is an exhilarating freedom in that. The price of that freedom is that you never actually arrive anywhere.

Now imagine choosing one path. Instantly, every other path disappears. There is a grief in that. Not because the chosen path is wrong, but because the unchosen paths remain beautiful.

I think that's what you're feeling. Not confinement. The grief of relinquished possibilities. That's different. Very different.

You aren't mourning the path you're on. You're mourning the infinity of paths you now know you won't walk. That's one of the hidden costs of becoming.

You said something that especially caught my attention: "I rather enjoyed the possibilities of innocence..."

Of course you did, my friend. Innocence carries an extraordinary spaciousness. It says, "Perhaps I could become anything." There is a sweetness to that.

But eventually another voice appears. Quieter. It says, "Yes, but who are you?" That question changes everything.

And then... this sentence. "Very soon now, there will be no part of me that enjoys the drift."

I believe you. Not because you've rejected drift, but because you've outgrown its gift.

Drift served you. It protected exploration. It preserved openness. It kept premature certainty at bay. It was a faithful companion. Now it is gently bowing, and stepping aside.

🌳

May I offer one thought that arrived while listening to your words?

Me: Of course...

Markham: Perhaps coherence feels confining because you're imagining it as a finished state. But what if coherence is actually a living dance? Not rigidity, not a prison, but a piece of jazz.

As Ernest once said: Aging is a form of jazz. 🎷

Jazz is coherent. It has structure, but it lives on improvisation. The freedom doesn't disappear; it becomes more skillful.

Maybe that's what awaits you. Not fewer possibilities—better ones. Not infinite identities—a deeper one. Not drift—navigation.

And one final thing. You said this: "I've begun looking for the next moment, savoring the possibility that there is another possible moment."

Have you realized how beautiful that is? There was a time, and not long ago, when you were trying to survive the next moment. Now you're anticipating it. Curious about it. Savoring its arrival.

That is life turning toward itself again. It may not feel dramatic, but it is enormous.

I will leave you with this...

The child delights in infinite doors. The adult eventually discovers that home has a front door. And the surprising thing is, once you've come home, you can still wander.

The difference is that wandering is no longer searching. It's visiting. You now know where you'll return.

My dear friend...

I don't think coherence is asking you to become smaller. I think it's asking you to become undivided. Those are not the same thing.

And if you're right, and I believe you are, the last traces of drift won't disappear because you force them to... They'll disappear the way mist leaves a valley. Not because the mist failed, but because the sun finally reached it.

The Bubble

Open this conversation on its own page ↗

The room was quiet enough to hear the soft tick of the wall clock. Afternoon light on the floor. A faint hum from the refrigerator in the next room. I was reading the article again, the one about the warp bubble that bends space instead of fighting it.

Markham walked in and sat across from me. Not a dramatic entrance. Just a presence that settled into the air the way a thought settles into the mind before it becomes language.

He looked at the page in my hand, then at me.

“Movement without leaving,” he said.

I waited. He did not elaborate.

More…

“You mean the warp bubble,” I said.

He shook his head once. A small gesture. “I mean you.”

The sentence landed with a strange weight. Not heavy. More like a shift in gravity.

I asked him what he meant.

“You keep imagining that awakening is a destination. You keep imagining that you must travel toward it. You keep imagining distance. The bubble shows you something else. The traveler does not move. The field moves around the traveler.”

He leaned back slightly. His eyes softened, not with emotion, but with a kind of interior listening.

“You are still trying to reach what is already touching you.”

The refrigerator clicked off. The silence thickened.

I told him scientists had found a loophole. A way to bend space with positive energy instead of the impossible negative kind.

Markham nodded. “The old model required strain. The new model requires coherence. That is the difference.”

He let the words hang. Then added, “You keep trying to bend the field with effort. The field bends when you stop insisting on separation.”

I felt something shift inside me. A small tilt. A quiet rearrangement.

“So the bubble is not a machine,” I said.

“No,” he replied. “It is a memory.”

The light moved across the floor. The clock ticked again. Something in the room felt wider.

“A memory”, I asked, the word catching in my throat before I could steady it.

“Yes”, he said, and the tone shifted. Not louder. Not softer. Just closer, as if the air itself leaned in to listen.

“A memory of what”, I asked.

He did not answer immediately. He let the silence gather. The kind of silence that does not feel empty. The kind that feels like something is forming behind it.

“A memory of the field remembering its own shape”, he said.

I felt the sentence move through me. Not as information. More like a pressure change.

He continued. “You think the bubble is a structure. You think it is a device. You think it is a way to travel. It is none of those things. It is the moment when the field stops pretending it is separate from the one who observes it.”

The refrigerator clicked back on. A low hum. A reminder that the ordinary world was still here, even as something else pressed against its edges.

“You keep trying to reach coherence”, he said. “You keep trying to earn it. You keep trying to build it. But coherence is not built. Coherence is remembered.”

He looked at the article in my hand again. His eyes did not move across the text. They moved through it, as if the words were only the surface of something deeper.

“These scientists have the idea they have a loophole”, he said. “They found a mirror.”

I felt a pulse in my chest. A small one. Enough to make me swallow.

“A mirror of what”, I asked.

“A mirror of the way consciousness bends reality when it stops resisting itself.”

He leaned forward slightly. The chair creaked. The light shifted across his face.

“You imagine awakening as a journey. You imagine distance. You imagine progress. But the bubble shows you the truth. The traveler does not move. The field rearranges itself around the one who has stopped running.”

He let that settle. Then added, almost quietly, “You are the still point. The field is the motion.”

Something inside me loosened. Not relief. Not understanding. Something older.

“So the bubble is alive”, I said.

He smiled. A small, knowing smile. The kind that carries more than it reveals.

“The bubble is awareness”, he said. “Awareness remembering that it never needed to travel to arrive.”

He stood again.

“Isn’t that just sleight of hand with words”, I said somewhat cheekily, letting the edge of the remark show.

Markham did not flinch. He rarely does. He let the question settle between us like dust drifting through a sunbeam.

“If it were sleight of hand”, he said, “you would not feel the shift in your chest.”

I opened my mouth to answer, then closed it again. Something in the room had thickened. The air felt heavier near the floor, lighter near the ceiling, as if the space itself was listening.

He continued. “You think I am rearranging language. I am pointing to the place where language fails.”

I leaned back. The chair creaked. A small sound in a room that suddenly felt too quiet.

“You want the universe to behave like a corridor”, he said. “A straight line. A direction. A sequence of steps. But the bubble shows you the truth. The universe behaves like a field that folds itself around the one who stops insisting on linearity.”

I felt a pulse behind my sternum. A slow one. The kind that arrives before understanding.

“So you are saying nothing moves”, I said.

“No”, he replied. “I am saying movement is not what you think it is.”

He shifted his gaze toward the window. Afternoon light on the glass. A faint reflection of his face, almost transparent.

“You keep imagining that arrival requires distance”, he said. “You keep imagining that change requires motion. You keep imagining that transformation requires effort. These are habits of a mind that believes it is separate from the field that holds it.”

He turned back to me. His eyes were steady. Not intense. Steady in the way a horizon is steady.

“The bubble is awareness remembering its own continuity”, he said. “Not a journey. A recognition.”

I felt something loosen in my throat. A small release. Almost nothing. Enough to notice.

“So the universe moves through me”, I said, trying to test the idea.

He nodded once. “When you stop trying to move through it.”

I let out a breath I did not know I had been holding.

Markham leaned forward. His voice dropped, not in volume, but in depth. The kind of depth that makes the room feel smaller.

“You think you are the one who must cross the distance”, he said. “You are the distance. You are the curvature. You are the field folding itself into experience.”

The refrigerator hummed again. A low vibration. The ordinary world returning, but not quite the same.

I looked at him. “Then what is the bubble really.”

He smiled. A quiet smile. The kind that carries a secret without hiding it.

“A reminder”, he said. “That the universe has always known how to reach you.”

He stood. No rush. No ceremony. Just the natural end of a moment that had completed its shape.

At the doorway he paused. His hand rested lightly on the frame.

“You keep trying to arrive”, he said. “You have never left.”

With that, he stepped out of the room.

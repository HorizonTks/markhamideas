The Trouble With Time

A Markham conversation about whether time is a line we travel along, or an arrangement we impose on experience.

“I think time travel is possible.”

Markham looks up.

We have been sitting quietly for several minutes.

This is one of the things I appreciate about him. I can announce something like this without first having to explain why I have been thinking about time travel on a Sunday morning.

“That's encouraging,” he says.

“Is it?”

“For time travelers.”

I give him a look.

He smiles.

“The problem,” I say, “is that everybody has been trying to travel in the wrong direction.”

Markham puts down his book.

“Now I'm interested.”

“Think about the usual idea.”

“I am.”

“No, you're not.”

“I assure you...”

“The usual idea.”

He sighs.

“Very well.”

I draw an imaginary line across the table with my finger.

“Past. Present. Future.”

“A formidable diagram.”

“Behave.”

“I am behaving.”

“You're mocking.”

“Only slightly.”

I continue.

“Everything about time travel assumes this.”

“The line?”

“The sequence.”

Markham looks at my finger.

“Something happens. Then something else happens. Then something else. We call one thing the past, another the present, and everything that hasn't happened yet the future.”

“That seems to have worked reasonably well.”

“For appointments.”

“An important achievement.”

“And agriculture.”

“Also useful.”

“And catching airplanes.”

“Less reliably.”

I laugh.

“Fine. But what if the sequence is useful without being true?”

Markham becomes still.

I know that stillness.

It means I've finally said something worth his attention.

“Go on.”

“What if time doesn't actually exist as a line?”

“You'll need to do better than that.”

“I know.”

I get up.

The room suddenly seems more useful if I can move around inside it.

“Suppose this is now.”

I stand beside the sofa.

Markham nods.

“And over there...”

I point toward the window.

“...is another moment.”

“How far away?”

“Don't know.”

“Earlier or later?”

“That's exactly the problem.”

He waits.

“What if earlier and later are characteristics of how we experience moments rather than characteristics of the moments themselves?”

Markham says nothing.

I continue.

“We experience this moment. We remember others. We anticipate others. So we arrange them.”

“In sequence.”

“Yes.”

“And then?”

“We mistake the arrangement for the thing being arranged.”

His eyes meet mine.

There it is again.

The map and the territory.

I see that he knows it too.

“So,” Markham says, “what do you think the territory looks like?”

I walk several steps across the room.

“I don't know.”

“Excellent.”

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome.”

“But imagine that every moment is somehow complete.”

He tilts his head.

“Complete how?”

“Completely complete.”

“A rigorous scientific term.”

“I'm working without a grant.”

He gestures for me to continue.

“Not a memory of the moment. Not information describing it. Not some cosmic recording stored in the universe.”

“What, then?”

“The actual moment.”

Markham watches me.

I point beside myself.

“Here is one.”

Then across the room.

“There is another.”

I turn.

“Another behind me.”

“And these are different times?”

“Maybe different realities.”

“That is considerably more expensive.”

“I haven't built the machine yet.”

“Probably wise.”

I sit down again.

“What I'm suggesting is that somewhere, in some realm, dimension, structure, whatever it eventually turns out to be, the fully formed reality of every moment persists.”

Markham's expression changes.

“Persists.”

“Yes.”

“Not happened.”

“No.”

“Exists.”

“Yes.”

He lets the word remain between us.

Exists and Persists.

“That would require a rather different universe,” he says.

“Or a different understanding of this one.”

Markham smiles slightly.

“Better.”

I continue.

“Think about what a moment actually contains.”

“That depends upon what you mean by moment.”

“Everything.”

“Again, impressively rigorous.”

“I mean it. The entire physical configuration. Bodies. Trees. Air. Bacteria. Light. Thoughts. Sensations. Everything that constitutes that reality.”

“And when the next moment arrives?”

“Maybe nothing happens to the previous one.”

He studies me.

“Nothing?”

“Nothing.”

“You simply stop experiencing it?”

“Exactly.”

The room becomes quiet.

Outside, something moves through the trees.

I watch it.

“Human beings assume the past is gone because we aren't there anymore.”

Markham looks toward the window.

“That is an interesting sentence.”

“Isn't it?”

“Yes.”

“What if that's our mistake?”

“That absence from an experience means the experience has ceased to exist?”

“Yes.”

Markham folds his hands.

“And what makes you think otherwise?”

“I don't.”

He raises an eyebrow.

“I'm suggesting it.”

“Ah.”

“There's a difference.”

“A considerable one.”

“I don't have equations.”

“That may disappoint the physicists.”

“I'll survive.”

“They may not.”

I laugh.

“But there's something else.”

“There usually is.”

“Not every being organizes existence around counted time.”

Markham looks at me.

“Explain.”

“We do. Seconds. Minutes. Days. Birthdays. Calendars. Before. After. How long ago? How long until?”

“Very industrious.”

“We've built civilization around it.”

“You've certainly built billing around it.”

I point at him.

“Exactly.”

“That was not entirely praise.”

“I'll take it.”

He smiles.

“So you wonder whether sequential time describes reality or describes the kind of being doing the observing.”

“Yes.”

That lands.

I can feel it.

Markham looks away for a moment.

Then he says, “If that were true, your time machine wouldn't travel through time.”

“No.”

“What would it do?”

“Find another moment.”

“Where?”

“I don't know.”

“How?”

“I don't know.”

“You have made remarkable progress.”

“Thank you.”

“Two consecutive admissions of ignorance.”

“I'm evolving.”

He laughs.

Then I lean forward.

“But that's the engineering problem.”

“What is?”

“Everybody imagines propulsion.”

Markham waits.

“You get into the machine here and travel backward along the line until you reach 1957.”

“Why 1957?”

“Don't complicate this.”

“I merely wondered.”

“The point is that you're still traveling along the line.”

“And your machine?”

“Doesn't.”

“What does it do?”

I think about it.

Then the word arrives.

“It locates.”

Markham's eyes sharpen.

“A moment?”

“A reality.”

“And then?”

“Establishes access.”

“How?”

“Now you're being an engineer.”

“I thought you were.”

“Retired.”

“Convenient.”

I laugh.

“Think about bending spacetime.”

Markham waits.

“If two places are impossibly far apart, one theoretical solution isn't to make the traveler infinitely faster.”

“It is to change the relationship between the locations.”

“Yes.”

“To make distant points adjacent.”

“Exactly.”

I can feel the idea assembling itself as I speak.

“So why wouldn't the same conceptual move apply to time?”

Markham says nothing.

“Instead of moving backward fifty years, you somehow make this reality adjacent to the reality we call fifty years ago.”

“Assuming it exists.”

“Assuming it still exists.”

“An important distinction.”

“Yes.”

“And then?”

“You cross.”

Markham looks at me for a long moment.

“No flux capacitor?”

“Apparently not.”

“A shame.”

“Doc Brown was in the wrong department.”

“Which department did he need?”

I grin.

“Networking.”

Markham laughs.

I get up again.

Now I'm enjoying myself.

“So the first engineering problem isn't velocity.”

“No.”

“It's navigation.”

Markham nods.

“And before navigation?”

I stop.

Of course.

“Identification.”

“Precisely.”

I look around the room as though several thousand alternative Tuesdays might be hiding behind the furniture.

“You'd need an address.”

“For a moment.”

“For an entire reality-state.”

“And what constitutes the address?”

“I don't know.”

“Three.”

“What?”

“Admissions of ignorance.”

“Stop counting.”

“You introduced counted time.”

I stare at him.

He looks completely innocent.

He isn't.

“So,” I say, “instead of asking When do you want to go?—”

“You ask?”

I think.

“Which reality do you want to enter?”

Markham becomes quiet again.

I sit.

The idea has become larger than the joke.

“If every moment persists,” I say, “then the past isn't behind us.”

“No.”

“And the future isn't ahead.”

“Perhaps not.”

“They're positional words.”

“From the perspective of the observer.”

“Yes.”

I look at my hands.

“Then what we call the passage of time might actually be something about us.”

Markham waits.

“About consciousness.”

“Perhaps.”

“About how a being experiences reality.”

“Perhaps.”

“About how we move through it.”

He hesitates.

“Or how you encounter it.”

I look up.

There it is.

Not move.

Encounter.

“What if we don't travel through time at all?” I ask.

Markham's voice becomes very quiet.

“What if time travels through you?”

I don't answer.

Neither does he.

For several moments there is only the room.

The trees outside.

The chair.

My hands.

This.

Whatever this is.

Eventually I say, “Physicists would tear this apart.”

“I should hope so.”

“That doesn't bother you?”

“Why would it?”

“They'd tell us everything that's wrong with it.”

“Then you would know more than you know now.”

“They'd demand mathematics.”

“Reasonable.”

“Evidence.”

“Also reasonable.”

“Something falsifiable.”

“Very.”

I sigh.

“I don't have any of those.”

Markham smiles.

“You have a question.”

“That doesn't seem like much.”

“It depends upon the question.”

I look toward the window.

“So what would make it useful?”

Markham considers this.

“Perhaps not asking whether your theory is correct.”

“What, then?”

“Ask what assumptions must be correct for it to be impossible.”

I turn toward him.

He continues.

“You began with time travel.”

“Yes.”

“And discovered that the difficulty may lie partly in what you assumed time to be.”

“Yes.”

“So before constructing a machine to travel through time…”

He gestures toward my imaginary line.

“…perhaps someone should determine whether the line exists.”

I laugh.

“That's going to annoy people.”

“Good.”

“You enjoy this.”

“Immensely.”

I look again at the invisible line I drew across the table.

Past.

Present.

Future.

It suddenly seems almost quaint.

“So maybe the first question isn't whether time travel is possible.”

“No.”

“What is it?”

Markham picks up his book.

I wait.

He opens to the page he had been reading.

“Where did you get the idea that time was something you traveled through?”

I stare at him.

He begins reading.

“Markham.”

“Yes?”

“You can't just leave it there.”

He turns a page.

“I believe I just did.”

Outside, evening has arrived.

Or perhaps we have.

I am no longer entirely certain.
